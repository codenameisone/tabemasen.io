Japan labels wheat, not gluten

Japan requires nine allergens to be declared on packaged food — the tokutei genzairyo (特定原材料) list: shrimp, cashew nut, crab, walnut, wheat, buckwheat, egg, milk and peanut. Cashew nut is the most recent addition, moved from the recommended list to mandatory on 1 April 2026, with full compliance required by 31 March 2028 (amendment to the Food Labelling Standards). The Consumer Affairs Agency minuted the decision at its Food Allergy Labeling Advisory Meeting of 21 January 2025 (USDA FAS GAIN JA2025-0006). A recommended list of 20 further items sits alongside it. Our labeling guide covers the change in detail.

Celiac disease is spelled coeliac outside North America; both spellings mean the same condition, and this page uses the American form throughout for consistency with the card generator.

Barley and rye are on neither that list nor the 21-item recommended list. Neither is “gluten” as a category. For most allergies this is irrelevant. For celiac disease it is the single most important structural fact about eating in Japan.

麦
A fast first pass, not a complete check. 麦 appears in 小麦 (komugi, wheat), 大麦 (ōmugi, barley), ライ麦 (raimugi, rye), 麦芽 (bakuga, malt) and 麦茶 (mugicha, barley tea) — one character catching five things at once, including the ones the allergen box will never flag. What it misses: 麩 (fu, dried wheat gluten, routine in miso soup and ramen) is a separate character that shares no part of 麦, and is often written ふ in kana. It also raises false alarms — はと麦 (Job's tears) and オート麦 (oats) are neither wheat, barley nor rye. And it only works where an ingredients list exists at all, which rules out most restaurant food.

The practical consequence: an allergen box with no 小麦 in it is not a gluten-free guarantee. Barley malt in a drink, barley in a mugi miso, roasted barley in the free tea — none of these trigger a mandatory allergen declaration, because barley is not a declarable allergen in Japan. They will usually still appear in the ordinary ingredients list, which is why the ingredients list, not the allergen summary, is the thing to read.

For how the labelling system works in full — the 10 ppm testing threshold, the recommended-list allergens, and why restaurants are exempt — see the Japan allergen labeling guide.

“Gluten-free” has almost no legal meaning in Japan

In the EU and the US, “gluten-free” is a regulated claim with a number behind it: the US FDA requires less than 20 ppm, and the European Commission 20 ppm or lower. Japan has no equivalent general standard.

It has one narrow scheme instead — and it is stricter than either. Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries issued “Labelling Guidelines to Promote Rice Flour Products” permitting rice flour containing 1 ppm or less of gluten to be labelled “Non-Gluten”, with third-party certification set up through the Japan Rice Flour Association (USDA Foreign Agricultural Service, GAIN report JA7094).

Three limitations matter more than the impressive threshold:

! It covers rice flour only — and only rice flour derived from Japanese rice. Not food in general, not restaurant meals.
! It is voluntary. The guidelines are not legally binding. Uncertified “non-glutinous” labelling does violate the Food Labelling Law, so the certification mark means something — but nothing compels a producer to seek it.
! The term itself is unfamiliar. The same USDA report records that “the term ‘gluten-free’ is not yet widely recognized in the Japanese market as the demand for gluten-free food remains small.”

So: treat グルテンフリー printed on a package or menu as a marketing claim, not a regulated one. The certification mark on rice flour is the only Japanese label with a defined gluten threshold behind it.

The MAFF guidelines and the USDA analysis of them date from 2017. The 1 ppm threshold and the rice-flour scope are what that report documents; whether the scheme has since broadened is worth checking before you rely on it.

Celiac disease is rare here, and that shapes every conversation

This is not a cultural aside — it is the reason the usual script fails. A 2023 review of celiac disease in Japanese patients records (Int. J. Mol. Sci., 2023):

Under 0.1% prevalence
A screening study found two biopsy-confirmed cases among 2,055 adults. Seropositivity in the same cohort was 0.19%.
HLA-DQ2 at 0.3–0.6%
Against 20–30% in Western Europe. DQ8 sits at 8–10%, similar to Western countries — the genetic picture is different, not simply absent.
27 documented cases
The total number of seropositive Japanese cases reported in the medical literature as of that review.
Limited clinical infrastructure
Serology is often not covered by health insurance, samples are frequently evaluated overseas, and few physicians and nutritionists have celiac expertise.

Two things follow. First, naming the diagnosis will usually not work. セリアック病 is a term many people — including some clinicians — will not have encountered. Naming the grains does work: 小麦, 大麦 and ライ麦 are concrete, everyday words. This is exactly why a written card lists the grains rather than announcing a condition.

Second, if you have a confirmed diagnosis, carry your own documentation. Do not plan on being re-diagnosed, or on a local clinician having ready access to celiac serology.

How to verify food yourself

Given all of the above, four habits do most of the work. These are checking techniques rather than a list of things to avoid — for the specific ingredients that catch people out, see where gluten hides in Japanese food.

✓ Read the ingredients list, not the allergen box. The allergen box is legally obliged to flag wheat and will stay silent on barley and rye.
✓ Scan for 麦. One character catches wheat, barley, rye, malt and barley tea.
✓ Ask for 十割そば — then ask about the water. Juwari soba is 100% buckwheat with no wheat binder (Food in Japan); most soba is not, because buckwheat is hard to bind without gluten. But a shop serving udon or nihachi soba alongside it will often boil everything in one pot, and that water is reused through service. Cooking water carries gluten: Coeliac Australia is explicit that water used for wheat pasta must be discarded before gluten-free pasta goes in. For a wheat allergy that is often survivable; with celiac disease it is the main risk in a soba shop, and it is worth asking before the noodles go in rather than after. The dipping sauce is a third question — tsuyu is soy-sauce based.
✓ Trust the mark, not the words. A “Non-Gluten” certification mark on rice flour has a 1 ppm threshold behind it. The text グルテンフリー has nothing behind it.

Common questions about celiac disease in Japan

Does “no wheat” on a Japanese label mean the food is gluten-free?

No. Japan's mandatory allergen list covers nine items — shrimp, cashew nut, crab, walnut, wheat, buckwheat, egg, milk and peanut. Barley and rye are on neither the mandatory list nor the 21-item recommended list, so barley malt or roasted barley can be present in a product whose allergen box shows no wheat. Read the full ingredients list, not only the allergen declaration.

Is “gluten-free” legally defined in Japan?

Not in the general way it is in the EU or the US, where a 20 ppm threshold applies. Japan's only formal scheme is narrower and stricter: MAFF's rice flour labelling guidelines permit rice flour containing 1 ppm or less of gluten to be labelled “Non-Gluten”. It is voluntary, requires third-party certification, and covers only rice flour made from Japanese rice. Outside that scheme, グルテンフリー on a package or menu is a marketing claim rather than a regulated one.

What is the Japanese “Non-Gluten” certification mark?

A voluntary mark for rice flour certified at 1 ppm gluten or below, administered through third-party certification set up by the Japan Rice Flour Association. The threshold is stricter than the US FDA's less than 20 ppm and the European Commission's 20 ppm or lower. Its scope is the limitation: it applies to rice flour from Japanese rice, not to food in general.

Will restaurant staff in Japan know what celiac disease is?

Often not. Celiac disease is genuinely rare in Japan — one screening study found two biopsy-confirmed cases among 2,055 adults, and the HLA-DQ2 carrier rate is around 0.3 to 0.6 percent against 20 to 30 percent in Western Europe. Saying セリアック病 may not communicate anything at all. Naming the grains does: 小麦 (wheat), 大麦 (barley) and ライ麦 (rye) are concrete and recognisable in a way the diagnosis is not.

Do Japanese doctors test for celiac disease?

It is not routine. A 2023 review notes that celiac serology is often not covered by Japanese health insurance, that samples are frequently sent overseas for evaluation, and that few physicians and nutritionists have celiac expertise. If you have a confirmed diagnosis, carry your own documentation rather than expecting to be re-diagnosed locally.

Is soba safe if I have celiac disease?

Only if it is juwari soba (十割そば), made from 100% buckwheat with no wheat binder — and even then, not automatically. Most soba blends wheat flour in, because buckwheat has no gluten and is hard to bind without it. Two further checks matter for coeliac disease specifically. First, the boiling water: shops serving udon or nihachi soba alongside juwari commonly use one pot, reused through service, and Coeliac Australia is explicit that water used for wheat pasta must be discarded before gluten-free pasta goes in. Ask whether the noodles are boiled separately. Second, the dipping sauce: tsuyu is soy-sauce based, and standard Japanese soy sauce is brewed with wheat.
